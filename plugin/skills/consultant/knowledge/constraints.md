# The constraint

Everything in the engagement hangs off one question:

Ask it plainly. The answer sorts the business into one of two worlds, and the two worlds get opposite plans.

## Demand constrained — "no, nothing breaks"

The business can absorb more customers today. response when a sticker-course owner said nothing would break: He then spent the entire session on offer and traffic, and explicitly skipped everything else: 

**Plan weighting:** offer first (so the traffic you buy converts better), then traffic volume. Conversion only if there's a visible leak.

**Typical moves:** raise the average order value so you can afford a higher CAC, then buy more attention. The order matters — fixing AOV first is what *permits* the spending increase. Telling someone to spend more before their economics support it is how you blow up a business.

## Supply constrained — "yes, something breaks"

More customers makes the business worse: delivery quality drops, the owner drowns, fulfillment slips, churn rises. Buying more leads here is actively destructive.

**Do not turn up traffic until capacity is solved.** This is the most common way a growth plan backfires.

### The four supply solutions

There are exactly four. Each one is a decision about what kind of company they're building — say that out loud, because owners pick one by accident and then resent the company they end up with.

| Solution | What it means | The business you're actually building |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Charge more, serve fewer, same revenue or better | A **branding** business. Supply stays fixed, so price only holds if demand keeps climbing — which means relentless brand and story reinforcement. |
| **Ratio** | Change delivery from 1:1 → 1:4 → 1:6 → 1:many | The **same** business, with more practice. You keep your X-factor; each customer gets less of your time but a more concentrated version of you as you improve. |
| **Productize** | Turn delivery into a product that runs without you | A **marketing and sales** business. Delivery is handled, so growth now depends entirely on acquisition skill. |
| **Hire** | Bring in people to deliver what you deliver | A **recruiting, training, and culture** business. This is the one people underestimate most. |

### Choosing between them

Work through them by elimination, out loud, the way does:

- **Price is out** if they already struggle to sell at the current price. A pricing solution usually needs a 3–4x move to matter, and you can't 3x a price you can barely defend. Price is *in* when buyers are wealthy, unbothered by cost, and the offer is genuinely differentiated.
- **Productize is out** if they already tried a productized version and couldn't sell it at a price that works. A very common trap: the owner runs out of capacity on the high-touch offer, builds a cheap low-touch offer to scale, and discovers the cheap offer costs the *same* to acquire a customer as the expensive one — which breaks the unit economics of the whole business. on exactly this: 
- **Hire is out** if the owner's value is hard to transfer. Blunt version: Most people train a replacement in a weekend, put them in front of customers, call it a system, and dilute the thing customers were paying for.
- **Ratio is usually what's left** for expert-led and service businesses — and it's underrated. It keeps the X-factor intact and gets *better* over time because the operator keeps improving.

**The shot-glass analogy** (use it, it lands): imagine your expertise as one full bottle. The hire model pours it into many glasses and tops each one up with water — everyone gets a full glass of diluted product. The ratio model gives each person a smaller glass of the undiluted thing. Most customers would rather have the real thing in a smaller pour. And as the operator improves, the shots get more concentrated, so value per customer holds even as you divide further.

Where the ratio breaks: past roughly **7 people in a group**, small-group stops feeling individual and becomes one-to-many, which is a different product and a different sale. Go from 1:1 to 1:4 deliberately rather than drifting.

## Two failure patterns to name on sight

**Solving the wrong problem with a new product.** When an owner responds to a capacity limit by launching something new, ask the two questions asks his own team: and The answer is almost always yes.

**Unlimited access as a feature.** Slack channels, "text me anytime", DMs answered personally. It reads as premium, it's usually the owner's favorite thing to offer and the customer's favorite thing to have — and it is a hard cap on the business. It's also self-reinforcing: fast replies train customers to ask more, which kills the community where those answers should live. Two useful counters:

- **Redirect the value.** Answer once, publicly, for everyone. 
- **Escalation lifelines.** Give them a fixed number of emergency calls per year — the "who wants to be a millionaire" lifeline frame. Reframe the removal honestly: unlimited access means the customer outsources every decision and never learns to prioritize. 

## Blocking beliefs

Supply constraints are frequently beliefs, not capacity limits. Owners "know" that their thing can't be delivered in groups, that their price can't go up, that their customers won't tolerate change. : 

When you hit one, don't argue it — find the counter-evidence in an adjacent industry and hand it over. Group relationship work happens in churches every week. Group medical education happens at dinner seminars. Someone in an adjacent market already does the thing they think is impossible.
