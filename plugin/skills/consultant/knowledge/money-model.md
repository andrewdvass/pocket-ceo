# Layer 2 — The money model

How and when cash arrives. A business can be profitable on paper and still unable to grow, because the cash comes back too slowly to fund the next customer. Fixing this often unlocks more growth than any tactic downstream.

## The core idea

Structure pricing and delivery so the profit from one customer funds the acquisition of the next several. Do that and the cash constraint disappears — you can outspend everyone for attention because you get the money back fastest.

Three stages, stacked:

1. **Get cash** — an attraction offer that acquires customers at or near breakeven
2. **Get more cash** — upsells and downsells that raise profit in the first 30 days
3. **Get the most cash** — continuity that maximizes lifetime value

## The number that actually governs spending

Not CAC. **What you make back in the first 30 days.**

When an owner is capping ad spend for no articulated reason — and they usually are — this is the unlock. Once they know 30-day recovery, the cap becomes a decision instead of an anxiety. Ask directly: The answer is almost always "no good reason," and naming that is worth more than any tactic.

**Net CAC is the honest math.** If you spend $3,700 to get 100 front-end buyers and those buyers immediately generate $3,500, your real cost to acquire the 22 who become customers is $200 — not $3,700. Owners routinely fail to see this and under-spend for years as a result. Do this arithmetic explicitly with their numbers; it changes behavior on the spot.

Track **EPC (earnings per click)** alongside CAC. It tells them whether they're fine, which is often the actual blocker.

## Pulling cash forward

When the cash conversion cycle is the constraint, these buy runway without raising price:

- **Deposit against the expensive part.** Charge the first quarter up front, justified honestly: the intensive onboarding is the most expensive thing you deliver. Then start the monthly payments immediately — don't leave a payment gap, that's where churn hides.
- **Layaway.** They can pay on any schedule they like, but delivery doesn't start until a threshold is met. Most people shrug and prepay. Keep an onboarding touch so it doesn't feel cold.
- **Downsell the layaway.** Preserves the sale without breaking the model.
- **Fast-action bonus.** Buy now, get the deep dive immediately and qualify for the next event. Ties urgency to something real.

## Annual over monthly

Pushing annual instead of monthly is one of the highest-return changes available, and it's usually a one-line change to a checkout page. In one case it took annual take-up from ~10% to 30% and doubled average order value — 

The aggressive version: **don't offer monthly at all during a launch.** Sell only annual with the bonuses, then run a mop-up campaign to monthly after the cart closes. People take the cheap option because you offered it.

**Physical product premium.** If customers need physical tools to get the outcome, bundle them into the annual tier and present them at full retail. A $149 kit that costs $20 landed as a free inclusion with annual is high-margin and highly persuasive — 

## Trials and continuity

The highest-leverage structural move for anything with a paid front-end event: **make the front-end a trial of the continuity offer.**

Instead of a $10 bootcamp converting 22% into a membership, the $10 bootcamp *is* a 7-day trial of the membership. Conversion goes to 100% by definition; the game becomes retention, and the whole event doubles as onboarding.

Math from the actual case — 100 front-end buyers:

- **As a bootcamp:** 22 convert × $300 LTV = $6,600
- **As a trial:** ~55 retain × $300 LTV = $16,500

Even with no upsells and no annual push, roughly a 50–60% lift. Keep the tool upsells on top.

Flag it honestly as the higher-risk test that it is, and de-risk it: run both at the same price point so the ads stay identical and only the funnel differs.

## Upsell timing

Sell **mid-flight**, not at the end.

The marathon analogy: buying shoes, you'll add a tank top and a phone band. After the race is over, you're done buying.

Cadence: **halfway, two-thirds, last chance.** Selling only at the end gets about a third of what's available. Selling through the process gets two-thirds or more. Owners resist this because it feels like asking too soon — the counter is that the customer has already received value.

**Renewals belong at live events.** Owners who "provide value and barely pitch" at their own events convert almost no one and then conclude events don't work. If you're going to pitch, pitch. Renewal incentives worth using: first-class flights to next year's events, or a straight discount for renewing on the day.

## Guarantees and refund control

A short no-questions guarantee raises conversion far more than it costs, and you frequently owe it legally anyway. It's especially load-bearing when a spouse or partner is an unrepresented decision-maker: 

Then kill the refunds with **speed**: onboard the next day. Deliver value before doubt arrives.

## Pricing tiers to route behavior

Price to steer people toward the path you want, and be honest about why.

A clean example: $2,500 to check out online, $3,000 if you want a one-on-one call first — Most self-serve; the ones who need hand-holding pay for it. If everyone chooses the expensive path, that's not a failure — that's a signal to raise prices.

Pair it with a bonus for buying on the call and a price that goes up after: **cheaper and more now, more expensive and less later.**
