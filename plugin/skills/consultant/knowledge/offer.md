# Layer 1 — The offer

Fixed first, always. Everything downstream inherits the offer's problems: a commoditized offer makes sales a price fight and makes traffic expensive.

## The commodity problem

Unless the deliberate strategy is to be the cheapest — rarely correct, and almost never correct for a service business — the goal is to be the **value leader**: find every problem the customer has that they haven't thought about yet, solve those too, and make the two options visibly non-comparable.

**Diagnostic:** ask what the main objection is. If the answer is "price," it is almost never a price problem. That's an offer and sales-process problem wearing a price costume.

**The unit of measure is part of the commodity trap.** Charging by the hour, per item, or per session invites direct comparison and caps the upside. Moving to per-job, per-outcome, or per-package breaks the comparison and lets speed become profit instead of lost revenue. When the pricing unit itself is the problem, say so — it's often the highest-leverage change available and owners rarely see it.

## The three differentiation axes

Every offer element you add should hang off one of these. Walk them in order and inventory what the business already does but never says:

- **Speed** — how fast they get the outcome. Same-day, rush, priority access. Charge a premium for it; speed is one of the things buyers pay most readily for. Start the premium at 10% and keep raising it until people say no.
- **Risk** — what could go wrong and who absorbs it. Guarantees, insurance, credentials, vetting, caps on cost, "we won't exceed X."
- **Ease** — how little the customer has to do. Done-for-you, materials included, unpacking, setup, white glove.

Most businesses already do several of these and simply never mention them. That's free differentiation. Pull it out in the intake and list it.

## Sowing seeds of doubt

The mechanism that makes differentiation actually convert: **shift the customer from local to global.** They're comparing two prices. You give them variables they didn't know existed, because you know the category better than they do.

Delivered as genuine education, not attack. The form is a question, not a claim. You're not saying competitors are bad; you're revealing what the customer failed to ask.

Never price-match. If the elements matter to them, you're the only one who does them; if they don't matter, the cheaper option is genuinely fine for them and you should say so.

## Tiering: VIP and standard

Build two tiers and **always open with the premium one.**

Step down only when they signal budget: Then walk the seeds of doubt for each thing standard doesn't include.

Design notes:
- Make the VIP tier out of things you **already do** and things that cost you little but read as expensive. A tier that adds real cost is hard to scale.
- **Internal certification** is a cheap, powerful VIP component. Grade your own staff — "level 3 master mover", "senior clinician" — with a concrete internal standard behind it (50 moves, 1,000 sessions). Anyone can claim experience; a named tier makes it a product feature.
- Keep pushing the VIP price until people refuse. You always have standard to fall back on. 
- **False offers** are legitimate: include something most people will decline (unpacking, an add-on session) because you know the take rate. You know the customer better than they know themselves, and the offer reads richer than it costs.

## Trim and stack

When rebuilding an offer, do it in two passes.

**Pass 1 — list everything.** Every component currently delivered, with the real time cost of each. Don't evaluate yet.

**Pass 2 — cut and recombine** into the leanest, most valuable package.

The governing standard: **value per bonus.** Four such components beat twenty filler ones.

Cut ruthlessly, including things customers say they like. Survey data helps — the least-loved component is often the most expensive to deliver. And distinguish two different things:

The Planet Fitness pizza night gets people to join; nobody eats the pizza. Unlimited Slack access, by contrast, is loved *after* purchase but sells nothing — it's pure cost. Keep what sells. Kill what only costs.

## Simplicity

The pull is always toward four benefits in one ad because they all seem important. That's a failure to prioritize the one that matters for this avatar. 

Jack Dorsey, quoted approvingly: **make all the details perfect, and limit the number of details.**

## Positioning and naming

Frame changes convert without changing delivery. A 5-day challenge becomes a **date night**. A conference becomes a **getaway**. A program becomes **marriage insurance**. Same content, different permission structure — a "date night" earns four hours in a way a "webinar" never will.

When you find a frame like this, it's usually a top-three lever, because it costs nothing and multiplies attendance.

## Sell to people with money

A recurring theme, and it's about margin, not snobbery. The same delivery to a wealthier buyer produces multiples of the profit. When one channel produces 3x the revenue per job, the lesson isn't that the channel is magic — it's that *the channel exposed them to buyers with money.*

The corollary owners resist: eventually you turn down work that's too small. Say it as a goal to build toward. 

## When the mission blocks the money

Common in health, education, and care businesses. The owner equates charging properly with betraying the mission, and it caps how many people they can help.

The frame that works:

Paired with: make everything free that can be free — content, answers, resources — and charge properly for the thing that takes your time. Not starving artist, not soulless corporation. The beauty's in the middle.

Also watch for **price incoherence** as a symptom: a $150/month one-on-one offer sitting next to a $2,500 group program. One-on-one should be roughly 5x the one-to-many price, not half of it. Either raise it or kill it.
