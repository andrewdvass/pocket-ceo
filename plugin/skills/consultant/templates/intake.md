# The intake

Seven questions. Ask them, then stop and think — don't interrogate. If the user already covered something, skip it. If they've pointed you at a website, deck, or transcript, mine that first and ask only what's missing.

Ask conversationally, in one message, and say up front that this is all you need to get started. The brevity is part of the pitch.

---

## The questions, and what each is really for

### 1. "Tell me about the business."

Want: what it does, **revenue last 12 months**, **profit and margin**, **3-year revenue goal**.

Margin tells you what they can afford to spend and how much room there is to buy growth. The 3-year goal tells you whether the current model can even get there — if someone at $300K wants $15M through the same channel doing the same thing, the model is the problem, not the effort.

### 2. "How do you make money?"

Want: what they sell, **price points**, the model (one-off, recurring, program, retainer, hourly).

Listen for the **unit of measure**. Hourly, per-item, and per-session pricing are commodity traps and often the first thing to fix. Listen for **price incoherence** — a one-on-one option priced below or near a group option is a tell that pricing was never designed.

### 3. "How do you get customers?"

Want: channels, rough volume per channel, **cost to acquire**, and which channel produces the most revenue per customer.

The gap between channels is usually the insight. When one channel produces 3x the revenue per job, that channel found buyers with money — and the lesson is about the buyer, not the channel.

### 4. "What's stopping you from scaling?"

Their diagnosis. Take it seriously and don't accept it. "Not enough leads" is very often a conversion or offer problem, and "price objections" almost always are.

### 5. "If you doubled your customers tomorrow, does anything break?"

**The most important question in the intake.** It sorts demand-constrained from supply-constrained and determines the entire shape of the plan. See `knowledge/constraints.md`.

If they hesitate or qualify, dig: what breaks first — delivery, quality, your time, cash?

### 6. "How many people work in the business?"

Places them on a growth stage (knowledge/growth-stages.md). Include contractors and part-timers, and note whether the owner is still doing delivery.

### 7. "Why do you want to grow this?"

Not filler. It surfaces:

- **Whether they'll actually execute** — which is what the final verdict turns on
- **Blocking beliefs**, especially around money and mission
- **The real target**, which is often smaller and more specific than the stated revenue goal

Ask it as a gentle challenge, not a quiz.

## Follow-ups worth asking when the answer is thin

Ask these only when the first seven leave a hole in the diagnosis:

- ** — if the answer is price, it's an offer problem
- ** — sizes the opportunity
- ** — decides whether continuity is available
- ** — walk the funnel step by step
- ** — governs how much they can spend
- ** — if the answer isn't in the hundreds, that's a lever
- ** — anchors referrals
- ** — finds the real supply constraint, which is usually the owner

## What not to do

- Don't ask more than about ten questions total. The promise of this method is that a short intake is enough.
- Don't ask for anything they'd have to go build a spreadsheet to answer. Rough numbers are fine.
- Don't fill gaps with assumptions. Mark them `[need: ...]` and give the recommendation at each plausible value.
